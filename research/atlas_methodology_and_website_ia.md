# JCX PropTech Intelligence Atlas: Research Methodology and Website Information Architecture

**Status:** working methodology for the research atlas and future website  
**Research baseline:** 30 August 2026  
**Owner:** JCX research/editorial team  
**Scope:** property technology, construction technology, real-estate operating technology, enabling infrastructure, relevant standards, investors/accelerators, and technology-enabled real-estate businesses

## 1. What this atlas is for

The atlas should answer four different questions without mixing their evidence:

1. **What exists?** Which companies, products, standards, investors, programs, and technology-enabled operators are relevant to the built environment?
2. **What does each entity actually do?** What user, workflow, asset, and economic problem does it address, and where does it sit in the real-estate lifecycle?
3. **How credible and ready is it?** What is known from primary evidence, what is merely claimed, and how deployable might it be in a JCX context?
4. **What should JCX do next?** Which entities deserve monitoring, a demo, a paid pilot, a partnership, a channel conversation, or no action?

The atlas is an intelligence product, not a vendor directory, investment recommendation, or certification. A listing means that an entity met the inclusion rule and has a traceable evidence record; it does not mean JCX endorses it or that performance will transfer to Bangladesh.

### Editorial principles

- **Evidence before narrative.** Every material factual statement has a source, publication date (if available), retrieval date, and claim type.
- **Separate facts, claims, interpretation, and recommendation.** The reader should be able to tell which sentence came from a company, which was independently reported, and which is the atlas team's judgment.
- **Lifecycle first; technology second.** A technology label alone does not explain where value is created or who must change behavior.
- **Context is part of the product.** A US office case study, a Bangladesh residential developer, and a global infrastructure standard are not directly comparable.
- **Uncertainty is visible.** Unknown is a valid value. Do not turn missing evidence into a low score disguised as precision.
- **No pay-to-rank.** Sponsorship, advertising, introductions, or access must never alter inclusion, tier, score, or editorial wording.
- **Version everything.** Taxonomy, scores, claims, and status are snapshots that can be updated without erasing the historical record.

## 2. Research protocol

### 2.1 Unit of analysis

The atlas uses separate but linkable records. Do not collapse these into a single “company” row:

| Record type | Definition | Typical evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Organization** | Legal or operating entity: startup, public company, developer, investor, accelerator, standards body, or association. | Official site, filings, registry, program page, reputable interview. |
| **Product / offering** | A named product, module, service, marketplace, or operating model offered by an organization. | Product documentation, pricing, demo, release notes, customer material. |
| **Case study** | A bounded deployment involving a named customer, asset, geography, period, and outcome. | Customer announcement, contract/public filing, independent report, vendor case study. |
| **Transaction / relationship** | Funding, acquisition, partnership, investment, implementation, or pilot. | Regulatory filing, investor/company announcement, reputable trade press. |
| **Standard / framework** | A maintained specification, assessment, protocol, or classification system. | Standards body or governing organization. |
| **Program / ecosystem** | Accelerator, venture fund, challenge, association, or research center. | Official program directory and cohort pages. |
| **Claim** | A single assertion that can be independently graded and revised. | One or more source citations plus extraction date. |

An organization may have many products, products may serve many lifecycle stages, and a case study must not be used as a proxy for all customers. Acquired products remain distinct records linked to the acquiring organization, with effective dates.

### 2.2 Discovery funnel

Research proceeds in four passes:

1. **Universe construction:** start with authoritative directories, public-company filings, standards bodies, accelerator directories, industry maps, trade events, procurement categories, and known customer ecosystems.
2. **Longlist expansion:** search by lifecycle problem, buyer/user, geography, asset class, and adjacent technology—not only “proptech.” Include companies that do not call themselves PropTech but materially serve real estate or construction.
3. **Verification:** open primary sources; capture the exact product, market, status, dates, and claims. Use secondary sources to discover leads or triangulate, not to silently replace primary evidence.
4. **Editorial review:** assign taxonomy, scores, evidence grades, confidence, and a reviewer; send material ambiguity to a second reviewer before publication.

For a priority record, target at least **three independent source classes**: (a) organization/product primary evidence, (b) customer, regulator, filing, or standards evidence, and (c) independent market or industry context. One source may satisfy more than one class only when it clearly serves both purposes. Early-stage companies can be listed with less evidence, but their uncertainty must be explicit.

### 2.3 Search and extraction log

Each research action should record:

- query or discovery path;
- source URL and source type;
- date accessed and publication date;
- country/region and language;
- exact page/section/quote or data point used;
- claim IDs supported or contradicted;
- researcher, reviewer, and review date;
- unresolved questions and next verification action.

Do not scrape around paywalls, robots restrictions, private databases, or personal data. Respect licensing and retain only the minimum excerpt needed for auditability. Link to the source rather than republishing copyrighted tables.

## 3. Atlas taxonomy

The taxonomy is deliberately two-dimensional: **where in the lifecycle** and **what technology layer**. Every product receives one primary lifecycle node and may receive multiple secondary nodes. Technology layers are orthogonal tags.

### 3.1 Lifecycle taxonomy

| Code | Lifecycle domain | Included problem families |
|---|---|---|
| **L1** | Land, site, and origination | Landowner/JV pipelines; title and parcel data; site search; zoning; permits; geospatial intelligence; environmental and infrastructure constraints; community engagement. |
| **L2** | Feasibility and development strategy | Highest-and-best-use; demand and rent/pricing models; scenario planning; generative feasibility; market intelligence; portfolio strategy; development approvals. |
| **L3** | Capital, finance, and transactions | Equity/debt marketplaces; underwriting; valuation; mortgage/origination; payment/escrow; investor reporting; tokenization/fractional models; transaction coordination. |
| **L4** | Design, BIM, and preconstruction | Authoring; coordination; clash detection; common data environments; digital requirements; estimating; takeoff; scheduling; embodied carbon and design optimization. |
| **L5** | Construction delivery | Procurement/RFQ; subcontractor management; project controls; field reporting; quality and safety; progress capture; change orders; payments; logistics; robotics and industrialized construction. |
| **L6** | Marketing, sales, leasing, and distribution | Listings; discovery; portals; virtual tours; lead capture; CRM; channel/broker management; pricing and revenue management; booking; digital signatures; lease administration. |
| **L7** | Occupancy and tenant/resident experience | Tenant/resident apps; amenity booking; community; access; concierge; work orders; payments; communications; experience analytics; affordable-housing and inclusion services. |
| **L8** | Property operations and facility management | Asset registers; CMMS/IWMS; preventive maintenance; inspections; utilities; BMS; space/workplace; security; cleaning; vendor management; service performance. |
| **L9** | Asset, portfolio, and investment management | Portfolio accounting; data rooms; asset performance; acquisition/disposition; benchmarking; risk; debt covenant; investor reporting; portfolio analytics. |
| **L10** | ESG, climate, resilience, and health | Energy, GHG, water, waste, biodiversity, embodied carbon; certifications; climate risk; resilience; indoor air/health; social impact; ESG data and assurance. |
| **L11** | Handover, warranty, and end-of-life | Digital handover; snagging/defects; warranty; decommissioning; circularity; salvage/material passports; demolition; reuse and redevelopment. |
| **L12** | Cross-lifecycle data and trust | Master data; identity; document control; interoperability; data exchange; cybersecurity; privacy; compliance; AI governance; records and provenance. |

The lifecycle reflects the fact that value and risk often move between phases. For example, BIM data created in L4 may only become useful when it survives handover into L8; an energy platform in L10 can be a procurement, operations, financing, or disclosure product depending on its buyer.

### 3.2 Technology-layer taxonomy

| Layer | Description | Examples of tags |
|---|---|---|
| **T1 System of record** | Core structured records and controlled workflows. | ERP, CRM, accounting, lease, project controls, IWMS/CMMS, document management. |
| **T2 Applications and workflow** | User-facing applications that execute a bounded process. | Site-visit scheduling, snagging, landowner pipeline, procurement, tenant app. |
| **T3 Data and intelligence** | Data collection, integration, analytics, AI, search, simulation, and decision support. | Geospatial, valuation, BI, machine learning, generative AI, digital-twin analytics. |
| **T4 Physical and connected asset** | Sensors, controls, equipment, devices, networks, and robotics deployed in or around assets. | BMS, meters, access control, drones, 360 cameras, construction robotics. |
| **T5 Immersive and visualization** | Spatial and visual interfaces. | BIM viewers, VR/AR, virtual tours, 3D/4D, digital twins, computer vision. |
| **T6 Market and financial rails** | Discovery, exchange, funding, payment, identity, and transaction infrastructure. | Marketplaces, mortgage APIs, payment/escrow, digital signatures, fractional models. |
| **T7 Enabling infrastructure and governance** | Cybersecurity, identity, cloud, APIs, standards, privacy, assurance, and professional services. | Open standards, data dictionaries, consent, encryption, interoperability, implementation. |

Cross-cutting tags should capture **AI involvement** (none, assistive, predictive, generative, agentic, autonomous claim), **deployment** (SaaS, on-premise, hybrid, hardware, managed service), **integration posture** (open API, exports only, proprietary/no public detail), and **data sensitivity** (public, commercial, personal, financial, location/safety critical).

### 3.3 Asset, buyer, and business-model tags

Minimum controlled vocabularies:

- **Asset:** residential for-sale, multifamily/rental, office, retail, industrial/logistics, hotel, student, senior living, healthcare, education, public/infrastructure, data center, land, mixed-use, construction project, portfolio.
- **Primary buyer:** developer, owner/operator, REIT/fund, lender/insurer, broker/agent, contractor, architect/engineer, facility manager, tenant/resident, government/city, supplier, investor, consumer.
- **Commercial model:** subscription, per-seat, per-asset, per-transaction, marketplace take-rate, hardware plus software, managed service, project fee, advertising, free/open source, investment/ownership, unknown.
- **Market reach:** local, national, regional, global; supported countries must be recorded separately from “target market.”

## 4. What belongs in the atlas

### 4.1 Inclusion criteria

Include an entity when **at least one** condition is true and the entity passes the evidence and relevance tests:

- it provides technology or technology-enabled services that materially affect a built-environment workflow, asset, transaction, or outcome;
- it is an enabling data, infrastructure, security, or standards provider with a credible real-estate use case;
- it is a major real-estate operator or service company whose operating model is itself technology-enabled and instructive;
- it is a fund, accelerator, standards body, research group, or public program that shapes PropTech adoption or capital allocation;
- it is an early-stage company from YC or another reputable accelerator whose stated problem sits in the taxonomy, even when commercial evidence is limited;
- it is a materially relevant customer, pilot, or case study that helps evaluate a product.

For a **JCX priority profile**, require a plausible use case in Bangladesh or a transferable operating context, an identifiable buyer/user, and enough evidence to evaluate a next action. For a **watchlist profile**, weaker evidence is acceptable when the signal is strategically important, but mark it as unverified.

### 4.2 Exclusion criteria

Exclude or place in an unranked appendix:

- generic software with no material real-estate/construction use case;
- agencies or consultants that only claim “digital transformation” without a productized or evidenced capability, unless they are a strategically important implementation partner;
- ordinary brokers, landlords, developers, or contractors with no technology distinction (they may still be included as market actors or case-study customers);
- duplicate local branches or resellers when the product owner already has a canonical record;
- companies known only from a directory with no current primary evidence;
- unsubstantiated “AI-powered” labels where no workflow, model use, or product evidence is available;
- speculative token, fractional, or investment offerings that cannot explain legal ownership, custody, liquidity, consumer protection, and applicable regulation;
- entities whose evidence consists only of promotional awards, anonymous reviews, or scraped profiles.

Do not erase a relevant but failed company. Use a **historical / inactive** state with a dated evidence note, because failures, acquisitions, and pivots are part of the intelligence.

## 5. Tiering and maturity

Tier and maturity measure different things. A large incumbent can have low product readiness in a specific module; a tiny startup can have a strong, verified pilot but limited scale.

### 5.1 Atlas relevance tiers

| Tier | Meaning | Publication treatment |
|---|---|---|
| **A — Core** | Directly relevant to JCX's priority workflows or infrastructure; enough evidence for comparison or action. | Full profile, scorecard, source register, and explicit next step. |
| **B — Strategic** | Important global/regional benchmark, adjacent capability, or likely future relevance. | Full or compact profile; action is monitor, benchmark, or discovery. |
| **C — Emerging** | Early-stage, weakly evidenced, or niche entity with a meaningful signal. | Watchlist card with uncertainty; no implied endorsement. |
| **D — Context** | Useful market actor, standard, fund, or historical example but not currently actionable for JCX. | Reference page or linked record; no comparative rank. |
| **H — Historical** | Acquired, inactive, pivoted, discontinued, or otherwise no longer operating in its prior form. | Preserve timeline and link successor/parent; do not count as an active competitor. |

Tier is a human editorial decision informed by relevance score and evidence quality; it is not a popularity ranking.

### 5.2 Product maturity / readiness (MRL)

Use a 0–5 readiness scale, with domain-specific evidence required:

| MRL | Label | Minimum interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| **0** | Concept / signal | Thesis, research, or announcement; no verified product deployment. |
| **1** | Prototype | Demonstrable prototype or closed test; workflow and owner are identifiable. |
| **2** | Pilot-ready | Deployable in a bounded pilot with implementation plan, support, and basic security/data controls. |
| **3** | Proven in bounded deployments | At least one named or auditable production deployment; outcome and scope are documented, even if not independently verified. |
| **4** | Repeatable | Multiple customers or sites, repeatable implementation, support model, integration/export path, and evidence of operational reliability. |
| **5** | Scaled / institutional | Multi-market or substantial deployment, durable governance, security/assurance appropriate to risk, financial/operating durability, and independently corroborated outcomes where material. |

Do not infer MRL from funding, valuation, press coverage, employee count, or a polished demo. Hardware, AI, finance, and safety products should have additional domain gates before receiving MRL 3 or above.

## 6. JCX relevance scoring rubric

The score prioritizes what JCX should investigate, not which company is “best.” Score each dimension 0–5, record a one-sentence rationale, and show **unknown** when evidence is insufficient. Calculate only when at least six of the eight dimensions have evidence; otherwise display “insufficient evidence” plus a provisional tier.

| Dimension | Weight | 0–5 question |
|---|---:|---|
| **Problem fit** | 20% | Does it solve a verified JCX pain point or strategic opportunity? |
| **Lifecycle leverage** | 10% | Does it improve a critical handoff, revenue bottleneck, risk, or trust point? |
| **Bangladesh / regional fit** | 15% | Can it operate within local language, regulation, infrastructure, payments, labor, and market conditions? |
| **Integration and data fit** | 15% | Can it coexist with JCX's ERP/CRM/data architecture, preserve ownership, and export records? |
| **Evidence of outcomes** | 15% | Are outcomes named, measured, attributable, and independently or customer corroborated? |
| **Implementation readiness** | 10% | Can JCX run a bounded pilot with realistic effort, support, security, and change management? |
| **Economics / repeatability** | 10% | Is there a credible cost, payback, scalability, and repeatable deployment path? |
| **Strategic option value** | 5% | Could this create reusable capability, defensibility, or a useful partner/channel? |

`JCX relevance (0–100) = Σ (dimension score ÷ 5 × weight)`

Suggested interpretation:

- **80–100:** prioritize diligence or paid pilot, provided risk gates pass;
- **65–79:** structured discovery, benchmark, or monitor with a defined trigger;
- **45–64:** useful context or watchlist; no commitment implied;
- **0–44:** low current fit, or insufficient evidence; do not call it a negative quality judgment.

### Example score (illustrative, not a real company)

“Sample Workflow Co.” is a hypothetical construction-progress evidence product. Scores: problem fit 5, lifecycle leverage 4, Bangladesh fit 2, integration 3, outcomes 3, readiness 3, economics 3, option value 4. Weighted score: `(5/5×20) + (4/5×10) + (2/5×15) + (3/5×15) + (3/5×15) + (3/5×10) + (3/5×10) + (4/5×5) = 68/100`. The correct output is **structured discovery**, not “recommended.” The Bangladesh-fit constraint and outcome verification are explicit next questions.

## 7. Evidence, claim, confidence, and freshness

### 7.1 Source-quality grades

Grade the source, not the company:

| Grade | Source quality | Appropriate use |
|---|---|---|
| **S1** | Primary, authoritative, and auditable: regulator/filing, standards body, signed customer or procurement record, official product documentation, named customer announcement. | Core factual claims; high confidence when scope is clear. |
| **S2** | Primary but self-reported or promotional: company website, press release, vendor case study, founder interview, accelerator profile. | Product description and company claims; outcomes must retain “reported by.” |
| **S3** | Independent reputable source: major business/trade publication, research institution, professional body, expert survey with methodology. | Market context, triangulation, independent corroboration. |
| **S4** | Structured database or directory with a clear provenance but limited verification. | Discovery and basic metadata; verify material claims elsewhere. |
| **S5** | User review, social post, anonymous discussion, unattributed listicle, or search snippet. | Lead generation only; never sole support for a published material claim. |

### 7.2 Claim-attribution grades

Each claim receives a claim grade independent of source grade:

- **C1 — Verified fact:** directly supported by an S1 source or two independent sources with matching scope and dates.
- **C2 — Corroborated fact:** supported by a primary source plus credible independent corroboration, but with a material limitation.
- **C3 — Reported claim:** clearly attributed to the company/customer/partner; not independently verified.
- **C4 — Analyst inference:** derived by the atlas team from cited facts; label “atlas interpretation.”
- **C5 — Unverified signal:** plausible but only supported by S4/S5 or an incomplete primary source; keep on watchlist.

Display claim badges beside the statement, not only in a hidden bibliography. Example: “The vendor reports a 30% reduction in rework [C3; vendor case study; scope and baseline not independently verified].”

### 7.3 Freshness and status

Store both `published_at` and `accessed_at`; do not confuse retrieval date with recency. Suggested review intervals:

| Record / claim | Review interval | Expiration behavior |
|---|---:|---|
| Active startup/product metadata | 90 days | “Needs refresh” badge after 90 days; hide from active comparisons after 180 days without confirmation. |
| Major public company, standard, or stable infrastructure | 180 days | Reconfirm annually; retain effective version/date. |
| Funding, acquisition, executive, pricing, regulation, or security claim | 30 days | Must be date-stamped; supersede rather than overwrite. |
| Case-study outcome | 180 days | Keep original period and scope; never present as current performance without new evidence. |
| Historical/inactive record | Annual or event-triggered | Preserve status date and successor relationship. |

Status values: `active`, `pilot`, `stealth/unclear`, `acquired`, `merged`, `pivoted`, `paused`, `inactive`, `dissolved`, `unknown`. “Unknown” means the atlas has not verified status; it does not mean failed.

### 7.4 Vendor-claim discipline

For every outcome claim, capture: baseline, denominator, period, geography, asset type, customer role, intervention, comparison method, implementation duration, exclusions, and who measured it. If any are missing, downgrade to C3 and state the limitation.

Never convert a vendor percentage into a JCX business case. Use it to formulate a pilot hypothesis and baseline plan. For AI claims, additionally record the human role, error/false-positive rate if available, model/data provenance, evaluation method, privacy/security boundary, and what action the model is permitted to take.

## 8. Identity, duplicates, acquisitions, and pivots

Maintain a canonical `organization_id` and aliases. Normalize legal name, trading name, domain, country, and parent. Do not merge entities solely because their names or founders are similar.

Rules:

1. **Same product, same owner:** one product record, aliases and domains linked.
2. **Reseller/implementation partner:** separate organization linked with `resells`, `implements`, or `integrates_with` relationship.
3. **Acquisition:** preserve pre-acquisition organization and product timeline; link `acquired_by` with announcement/effective dates; parent becomes current owner only from effective date.
4. **Merger:** preserve both prior records and create the successor when a new legal/product identity exists.
5. **Pivot:** keep the historical product and add a new product/version when target buyer, workflow, or category materially changes; add `pivoted_from` and date.
6. **Shutdown:** retain the record as historical; archive live links only after recording the last known date and source.
7. **Name change:** alias, not a new organization, unless legal entity and operations are genuinely distinct.

Every merge or status transition requires reviewer, date, reason, and source. Historical scores are immutable snapshots; current scores may change with evidence.

## 9. Geography and comparability

Use ISO country/region fields plus a free-text operating-context note. Distinguish:

- headquarters, incorporation, founding, and current operating locations;
- target market, supported market, and verified customer market;
- data-hosting location and deployment region where relevant;
- local partner versus direct operation;
- language, currency, payment rails, title/land regime, building codes, labor model, and internet/utility assumptions.

Comparisons should be grouped by **context clusters**, for example: Bangladesh/South Asia residential development; GCC smart buildings; North American institutional CRE; European ESG/reporting; global BIM standards. A global score should not conceal a local implementation mismatch.

## 10. Metrics and outcome model

Metrics are only useful when their unit, denominator, period, and owner are known. Prefer operational metrics to vanity metrics.

### Atlas-level metrics

- number of active organizations/products by lifecycle, layer, geography, asset, and maturity;
- share of records with an S1/S2 primary source, independent corroboration, current review, and named buyer;
- number of additions, status changes, acquisitions, pivots, and removals per period;
- median age of evidence and percentage of stale records;
- score distribution by category, not only an overall “top companies” list;
- coverage gaps: lifecycle nodes, geographies, asset types, and buyer roles with fewer verified records;
- disagreement rate between reviewers and number of corrections after publication.

### Product/case-study metrics

- adoption and active-user rate by role;
- time-to-value, implementation duration, and support burden;
- data completeness, freshness, duplicate rate, and reconciliation effort;
- cycle time, first-response time, schedule variance, defect closure, rework, cost variance, energy/water intensity, incident rate, or conversion—only where the denominator and baseline are documented;
- customer experience, accessibility, complaints, and service-level adherence;
- security/privacy incidents, downtime, data-export success, and model error where relevant;
- realized financial value versus total cost of ownership, including integration and change management.

### JCX pilot measurement rule

Every pilot record needs a baseline period, intervention scope, comparison group or pre/post design, leading indicators, lagging indicators, owner, data source, stop/scale criteria, and a benefits-realization review. “Engagement” alone is not proof of value.

## 12. Update and quality-control process

### Monthly

- ingest announcements, filings, accelerator cohorts, standards updates, acquisitions, pivots, and local market signals;
- run stale-record queue and broken-link check;
- tag all additions as `unreviewed` until a reviewer assigns evidence and inclusion status.

### Quarterly

- refresh A-tier profiles and priority products;
- recalculate scores only when evidence or rubric changes, showing before/after and reason;
- conduct duplicate, alias, parent, and status audit;
- sample 10% of published claims for source-to-text traceability;
- review coverage gaps and search strategy.

### Annual

- version taxonomy and rubric; publish change log;
- review source hierarchy and conflicts-of-interest policy;
- archive inactive and superseded records without deletion;
- perform an external editorial review of high-impact profiles;
- publish a methodology note and corrections log.

### Review controls

Two-person review is required for: score ≥80; negative or reputational claim; safety, health, financial, legal, or regulatory claim; acquisition/shutdown status; case-study outcome presented as verified; and any recommendation to invest, procure, or run a pilot. Give companies a factual correction channel with a fixed response window; corrections must be logged, not silently substituted.

## 13. Website information architecture

The website should feel like a modern intelligence product: editorially led, visually confident, fast, and transparent enough for an executive to audit a claim in one click.

### 13.1 Primary navigation

1. **Atlas** — searchable universe with lifecycle map and filters.
2. **Signals** — dated trends, funding/acquisition/pivot/status changes, standards updates, and local-market signals.
3. **Themes** — curated essays such as “ERP-to-asset-data spine,” “NRB trust,” “construction assurance,” “climate readiness,” or “AI after data quality.”
4. **Compare** — side-by-side products, companies, standards, or case studies with context warnings.
5. **Case studies** — evidence packets, not promotional testimonials.
6. **Standards & playbooks** — taxonomy, standards register, pilot canvas, diligence checklists, and measurement templates.
7. **Methodology** — this document in an approachable, interactive form.
8. **About / corrections** — editorial team, funding, conflicts, contact, correction and takedown process.

Above the fold:

- “freshness” and “evidence coverage” indicators;
- editorially selected signal cards, not a random feed;
- quick paths: **Find a workflow**, **Compare**, **See what changed**, **Understand our evidence**.

Results should support cards and table views. Cards show identity, primary use case, lifecycle, geography, tier, MRL, JCX score (or “insufficient evidence”), evidence badge, and last reviewed. Never sort by score alone by default; default to relevance plus evidence coverage or let the user choose.

### 13.4 Filters

Filters should be composable and URL-addressable:

- lifecycle domain and subproblem;
- technology layer and AI mode;
- asset type;
- buyer/user;
- geography: HQ, supported market, verified deployment;
- entity type and business model;
- maturity/readiness;
- atlas tier and JCX score range;
- evidence quality, claim grade, last reviewed, source count;
- status, including historical/acquired/pivoted;
- integration: API, export, standard support, SSO, data residency;
- sustainability, resilience, health, accessibility, and cybersecurity tags;
- language and local partner availability.

Avoid “top 10” as the only discovery pattern. The interesting output is often a small set of complementary products across one workflow.

## 14. Page templates

### 14.1 Organization profile

Required sections:

1. **Identity:** canonical name, aliases, organization type, parent/child, founded/incorporated if verified, headquarters, operating markets, active status.
2. **One-line description:** plain-language proposition, attributed if sourced from the organization.
3. **Why it matters:** atlas interpretation, clearly labeled.
4. **Products and lifecycle map:** linked product records; primary/secondary nodes; buyer and asset tags.
5. **Business model:** pricing visibility, deployment, implementation, data ownership, integrations.
6. **Evidence panel:** source quality distribution, last reviewed, open questions, claim grades.
7. **Readiness and relevance:** MRL, JCX relevance score, dimension breakdown, confidence, next verification step.
8. **Customers/case studies:** only with source, period, scope, and attribution.
9. **Relationships:** investors, accelerator, partners, parent, acquisitions, competitors, standards.
10. **Risks and diligence:** security, privacy, regulatory, lock-in, local fit, support, financial durability.
11. **Timeline:** founded, launch, funding, major releases, acquisitions, pivots, status changes.
12. **Sources and corrections:** claim-level citations and revision history.

### 14.2 Product profile

Separate product facts from organization facts. Include workflow diagram, required inputs, outputs, users, integrations, implementation stages, permissions, data retention/export, interoperability, reliability/security evidence, pricing model, outcome claims, comparable alternatives, and pilot design. A product profile should answer “what would JCX have to change to use this?”

### 14.3 Case-study template

Use a standardized “evidence packet”:

- customer and vendor, role, asset type, geography, and period;
- baseline problem and measured denominator;
- intervention, implementation duration, and affected users;
- outcome metrics with units and comparison method;
- costs, constraints, side effects, and what did not work;
- source attribution and verification grade;
- transferability assessment to JCX (locality, scale, data, process, regulation);
- open questions and a suggested JCX pilot test;
- “not a guarantee” note when outcomes are vendor/customer reported.

### 14.4 Signal / editorial story template

Every story should distinguish **signal → evidence → implication → decision**:

- signal headline and date;
- what changed and why it is non-trivial;
- primary evidence and conflicting evidence;
- which lifecycle nodes and stakeholders are affected;
- local/Bangladesh implications and what is not transferable;
- companies/products/standards linked, without pay-to-rank;
- suggested questions or pilot hypotheses;
- confidence, freshness, and source trail.

## 15. Comparisons and interactive visualizations

### Recommended views

- **Lifecycle × technology matrix:** reveals crowded categories and whitespace; click through to records.
- **Company/product constellation:** map entities by lifecycle and layer, with size by evidence coverage—not funding or popularity.
- **JCX workflow map:** lead → visit → booking → documentation → construction → handover → care; overlay relevant products and data owners.
- **Maturity versus evidence scatter:** x-axis MRL, y-axis evidence quality, with bubble color for geography/context. Do not imply market value from position.
- **Timeline:** funding, launch, acquisition, pivot, standard version, case-study period; use event-level sources.
- **Portfolio coverage heatmap:** JCX strategic priorities against products, gaps, dependencies, and risk gates.
- **Evidence waterfall:** how many claims are C1/C2/C3/C4/C5 and how many need refresh.
- **Comparison table:** normalized columns for buyer, workflow, deployment, integrations, data/export, geography, maturity, evidence, score, and unknowns.

### Visualization safeguards

- provide a text/table alternative and keyboard navigation;
- include units, denominators, date ranges, source links, and methodology in the same view;
- show missing values as “unknown,” not zero;
- avoid map bubbles that suggest geographic scale when only headquarters are known;
- use color-safe palettes and do not rely on color alone for tier/status;
- preserve historical snapshots and show when a score changed;
- make charts downloadable only with attribution and current filters embedded.

## 16. Trust, disclosure, and disclaimer design

Trust should be part of the interface, not a legal footer. Add a small evidence badge to every card and a “How we know” drawer to every profile. The drawer should expose source, date, claim grade, limitations, and reviewer.

Required site-wide disclosures:

- The atlas is informational research, not investment, legal, tax, lending, engineering, procurement, safety, or cybersecurity advice.
- A company/product listing is not an endorsement, certification, customer reference, or guarantee of performance.
- Vendor/customer outcomes are labeled and may not be independently verified or transferable to JCX.
- Scores express current JCX research priorities and evidence, not intrinsic company quality or investment value.
- Data can be incomplete, stale, disputed, or subject to source licensing; unknown is not negative.
- Conflicts, sponsorships, introductions, and commercial relationships are disclosed on relevant pages.
- Users should conduct legal, security, privacy, financial, technical, regulatory, and reference diligence before acting.

Correction workflow: visible “Report a factual issue” link; intake ID; triage within five business days; material correction note and changelog; original claim preserved with a superseded marker. Do not remove negative information merely because an organization objects; correct it when evidence supports correction.

## 18. What to learn from existing PropTech databases—and what to avoid

Useful discovery and benchmarking sources include accelerator directories such as YC, venture databases such as PitchBook/Crunchbase/Dealroom/Tracxn, market-intelligence products such as CB Insights, startup maps from CREtech/MetaProp and regional ecosystems, and review/catalogue sites such as G2 and Capterra. They are useful for breadth, aliases, funding chronology, category vocabulary, and finding candidates.

The atlas should avoid their common failure modes:

- presenting a scraped profile as current truth;
- conflating funding, valuation, employee count, web traffic, awards, or reviews with product quality;
- collapsing a parent, reseller, product, and customer into one entity;
- using a single broad “PropTech” category that hides lifecycle and buyer differences;
- ranking by popularity without showing evidence, geography, or data coverage;
- carrying stale “active” statuses and unverified customer logos;
- repeating vendor case-study percentages as independently established outcomes;
- hiding methodology behind a paywall or selling rank position;
- using AI-generated summaries without claim-level source links and human review;
- removing failed or acquired companies and thereby erasing market history.

The atlas can be more valuable than a larger database by making provenance, context, uncertainty, and actionable next steps visible. Breadth is a discovery feature; judgment and evidence are the product.

## 19. Initial build sequence

1. Freeze taxonomy v1.0, evidence rubric, score rubric, and controlled vocabularies.
2. Create a canonical seed universe across JCX workflows, Bangladesh/South Asia, global benchmarks, standards, investors, accelerators, and historical examples.
3. Build source and claim records before writing polished profiles.
4. Research 25–40 A/B records across at least six lifecycle domains to test taxonomy coverage; do not optimize the UI around only sales or listings.
5. Run reviewer calibration on five deliberately difficult records: acquired company, vendor-only case study, YC early-stage company, standards body, and local reseller.
6. Ship Atlas MVP with search, filters, evidence drawer, profile, comparison, methodology, and corrections flow.
7. Add editorial theme pages and visualizations after the underlying source/claim model is stable.
8. Establish monthly ingestion, quarterly review, annual methodology/version release, and a public changelog before launch.

### Definition of done for a publishable priority profile

- canonical identity and status resolved or explicitly unknown;
- lifecycle, layer, asset, buyer, geography, and business model tags assigned;
- product facts separated from organization facts;
- at least three source classes or an explicit early-stage exception;
- all material claims have claim grade and source link;
- MRL and JCX score include rationale and unknowns;
- implementation, integration, security/privacy, and local-fit questions captured;
- no unsupported “leader,” “best,” “proven,” or “AI-powered” language;
- reviewer and last-reviewed date visible;
- correction and supersession path tested;
- profile can be compared without implying false equivalence.

## 20. Closing position

The atlas should become JCX's institutional memory for technology decisions: a living map of workflows, products, standards, evidence, and unanswered questions. Its most defensible advantage will not be claiming to list every company. It will be showing, with unusual clarity, **which problem a technology solves, for whom, under what conditions, with what evidence, and what JCX should test next**.
